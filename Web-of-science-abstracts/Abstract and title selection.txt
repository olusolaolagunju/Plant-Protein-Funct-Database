Abstract and title selection


Here is how I would do this, step by step, in plain terms.

**Step 1: Fix the labeled data first**
- Keep all 170 real relevant papers. Do not shrink this.
- Keep all 30 real irrelevant papers.
- Generate synthetic irrelevant papers in small batches (20 to 30 at a time), not all at once. After each batch, read them and remove any that are too similar to each other or too obviously wrong.
- Stop around 100 to 120 synthetic irrelevant papers. Do not force it to 140. Quality matters more than hitting a number.
- End state: about 170 relevant, about 130 to 150 irrelevant (real plus synthetic). This is close to balanced, which is good for training.

**Step 2: Hold out a clean test set**
- Before doing anything else, set aside 40 to 50 papers (mix of real relevant and real irrelevant, no synthetic) that you never use for training or prompt examples.
- This is your only trustworthy way to check if the model actually works, instead of checking accuracy on the same kind of data it learned from.

**Step 3: Build DPO training pairs**
- For each paper in your training set, make one pair: the correct label with correct reason, and a wrong label with a wrong-but-believable reason.
- Make the wrong reason realistic. It should look like a mistake a careless reader would make (for example, seeing "protein" and "solubility" and missing that the source is whey, not plant).
- This gives you roughly 300 pairs (170 plus 130, before counting held-out test papers).

**Step 4: Fine-tune a small open model, not Qwen 72B**
- Use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. It is small enough to fine-tune well with 300 pairs, and it performed well as a judge model in the survey.
- Use LoRA or QLoRA (light fine-tuning, not full fine-tuning). This fits on one GPU and needs less data to work well.
- Train with DPO using your pairs from Step 3.

**Step 5: In parallel, set up few-shot prompting with a closed model**
- Pick 15 to 25 of your best labeled examples (mix of easy and hard cases, including some synthetic near-misses). Do not paste all 200 into every prompt, it costs too much and is not needed.
- Use these as fixed examples in every prompt sent to GPT-4 or Gemini for classification.
- This gives you a second, independent classifier to compare against your fine-tuned model.

**Step 6: Compare both models on your held-out test set from Step 2**
- Run both the fine-tuned Qwen model and the few-shot GPT/Gemini model on the same 40 to 50 held-out papers.
- Check accuracy, and also read a few of the wrong answers by hand to see what kind of mistakes each one makes.
- Pick whichever works better, or use both and compare where they disagree (disagreement is often a sign of a genuinely hard paper worth double-checking by hand).

**Step 7: Classify the remaining papers**
- Use your chosen classifier (or both) on the rest of your papers.
- Anything both models agree is irrelevant, drop.
- Anything both agree is relevant, keep.
- Anything they disagree on, review by hand. This should be a small number if your classifier is good.

**Step 8: Score the relevant papers**
- For each paper that passed Step 7, score it on your four criteria (functional property, protein source, extraction method, modification method).
- Run this scoring 3 to 5 times per paper and average the score, since this was already shown to reduce random error in the survey.
- Require a written reason for the score every time, naming exactly what is missing.

**Step 9: Set your threshold**
- Look at the score distribution across a sample of maybe 50 papers.
- Pick a cutoff that keeps papers with at least 3 of the 4 criteria, then manually spot check 10 to 15 papers just above and below your cutoff to make sure the line makes sense.

This gives you two things for your dissertation: a working pipeline, and a real comparison (fine-tuned small open model versus few-shot large closed model) that is worth reporting as a result on its own, not just a means to an end.
