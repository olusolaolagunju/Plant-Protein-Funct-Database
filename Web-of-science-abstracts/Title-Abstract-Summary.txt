Conjugate = confirmed covalent bond. A new, permanent chemical bond has formed between the protein and the other molecule (sugar, polyphenol, polysaccharide, whatever it is), typically confirmed through direct evidence: FTIR showing new bond signatures, XRD showing structural changes consistent with covalent linkage, mass shift via SDS-PAGE, explicit grafting degree measurements, or the abstract stating the mechanism directly (Maillard reaction, glycation, oxidation-induced cross-linking).

Complex = non-covalent association, or unconfirmed/inferred bonding. The two molecules are physically associated, electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interaction, but no new covalent bond is formed, or the paper doesn't provide direct evidence that one formed, even if it uses hedged language like "potential complex formation."

Deamidation: converts glutamine/asparagine residues into glutamate/aspartate by hydrolyzing off the amide group (releasing ammonia). It does not form a conjugate, no external molecule gets attached, it's a structural rearrangement of the protein's own existing residues. Deamidation increases negative charge (which is consistent with this abstract's finding of decreased zeta potential).

Phosphorylation: attaches a phosphate group to specific amino acid residues (typically serine, threonine, or tyrosine). This is a conjugation-type reaction, a new covalent bond forms between the protein and the phosphate group. It's usually done enzymatically (via a kinase) or chemically (using a phosphorylating reagent like sodium tripolyphosphate, STPP, common in food protein modification). The abstract doesn't specify which method was used here, so I'd log it as phosphorylation (mechanism not specified: enzymatic or chemical).

Glycosylation: same as your other glycosylation papers, covalent sugar-protein conjugate via Maillard reaction, confirmed conjugate.

transglutaminase crosslinking: transglutaminase (TG/TGase) is an enzyme that catalyzes an acyl transfer reaction between two specific amino acid side chains: it takes the gamma-carboxamide group of a glutamine residue (the "donor") and links it to the epsilon-amino group of a lysine residue (the "acceptor"), on the same protein chain or between two different protein molecules. This forms a new covalent bond called an isopeptide bond (specifically, an ε-(γ-glutamyl)lysine bond), and releases ammonia as a byproduct.


So the rule I'd apply here, refined from the blanket approach: for reactions that are covalent by definition of the chemical name itself (phosphorylation, acetylation, succinylation, glycation/Maillard when explicitly named, TG crosslinking), naming the reaction is sufficient confirmation, no additional "covalent" language is required.


Acylation (acetylation/succinylation) works by masking the positive charge on lysine's amine group, converting it to a negative or neutral group. This increases the protein's overall net negative charge, which increases electrostatic repulsion between protein molecules, this is what typically improves solubility and reduces unwanted aggregation. It's a charge-modification strategy.
Guar gum conjugation works by attaching a large, bulky, highly hydrophilic polysaccharide onto the protein surface. This creates steric hindrance (physically blocking protein molecules from clumping together) and dramatically increases the protein's water-binding capacity, since guar gum itself is an excellent water-holder. It's a steric/hydration-based strategy, a fundamentally different mechanism than charge masking.


question:
when calcium already performed coagulation. what sort of arrangement is required again using deamidation;

answer:
Here's the mechanism the paper describes: deamidation converts glutamine/asparagine side chains into glutamate/aspartate, which adds new carboxyl groups to the protein. The paper states this explicitly: "increased carboxyl groups facilitating calcium bridging." Calcium ions (Ca²⁺) form bridges by binding to two negatively charged carboxyl groups on neighboring protein chains simultaneously, cross-linking them together into a gel network, that's literally how calcium-induced gelation works mechanistically. So more carboxyl groups available (from deamidation) means more sites for calcium to bridge between, which is why deamidated protein gels at a much lower concentration (9% down to 3%) and tolerates far more calcium (20mM up to 40mM) before over-aggregating.

So the arrangement isn't "deamidation instead of calcium" or "deamidation as an alternative gelation method," it's deamidation as a pre-treatment that makes calcium-induced gelation work better, by chemically preparing the protein with more of the exact functional groups calcium needs to bridge. This is the same "combined treatment, sequential dependency" pattern you've seen several times now (heat-then-GDL, pH-shifting-then-TG, hydrolysis-then-ultrasound-then-TG), just with deamidation and calcium bridging specifically.
