protein-polysaccharide conjugation

When conjugation does occur: Maillard glycation
This is a chemical reaction between:
Carbonyl group (C=O)
from a reducing sugar or a polysaccharide with a reducing end
and
Amino group (NH₂)
from lysine, arginine, or the N terminal amino group of a protein.
This produces a covalent protein–polysaccharide conjugate.
🧪 3. Clean mechanism of Maillard conjugation (food protein context)
Step 1 — Schiff base formation (initial condensation)
Carbonyl group (C=O) of reducing sugar
•	
Amino group (NH₂) of protein → Schiff base (C=N) This is reversible and occurs early.
Step 2 — Amadori rearrangement (stabilization)
Schiff base → Amadori product This is the stable, covalent glycation product.
Step 3 — Advanced glycation (optional, depends on heat/time)
Further rearrangements → cross linked structures, browning compounds, etc.
In food protein chemistry, we usually stop at Amadori products, because they:
•	improve solubility
•	improve emulsifying capacity
•	improve thermal stability
•	reduce allergenicity
This is true conjugation — a covalent bond between protein and polysaccharide.
🌾 4. Which polysaccharides can conjugate?
Only those with reducing ends:
•	dextran (reducing end)
•	starch hydrolysates
•	maltodextrin
•	glucose polymers
•	pectin fragments with reducing ends
•	lactose, glucose, fructose (not polysaccharides but reducing sugars)
These can form protein–polysaccharide conjugates via Maillard glycation.
🌱 5. Which polysaccharides cannot conjugate?
Non reducing polysaccharides:
•	guar gum
•	κ carrageenan
•	xanthan
•	gellan gum
•	locust bean gum
•	alginate
•	cellulose derivatives
These lack reactive carbonyl groups, so they cannot form covalent conjugates.
They only form complexes.
